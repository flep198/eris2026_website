NGC 660: HI Absorption Imaging

Data

In this tutorial we will image HI absorption from the central region of the nearby starburst galaxy NGC 660 at z = 0.002842.

The data were obtained using the European VLBI Network (EVN). We shall use the self-calibrated dataset for this tutorial since you have already learnt continuum imaging and self-calibration in the previous tutorials.

One important point to remember when imaging continuum emission from spectral-line datasets is that strong line channels should be excluded from the continuum model. If the spectral line is clearly visible in a standard plotms inspection, those channels should not be included when constructing the continuum image.

Absorption Lines

Unlike TW Hya, this dataset contains a spectral line seen in absorption rather than emission.

For an absorption line to be observed, a bright background continuum source must be present. This is one reason why continuum imaging is often an essential first step in absorption-line studies. The foreground gas absorbs a fraction of the background continuum emission at frequencies corresponding to the spectral transition.

In extragalactic radio astronomy, the continuum source is often associated with AGN activity in the central regions of galaxies. In the case of NGC 660, the compact radio source is particularly interesting because it appeared only after 2008, suggesting the recent onset of nuclear activity.

The EVN observations used in this tutorial were analysed by Argo et al. (2015), who used HI absorption to study the gas surrounding this newly active radio source.

One important difference between emission-line and absorption-line cubes is that absorption appears as negative flux density relative to the continuum level. Consequently, when inspecting images and spectra in CASA or CARTA, genuine absorption features often appear as negative features rather than positive peaks.

HI 21-cm Line

Neutral atomic hydrogen (HI) is the most abundant element in galaxies and serves as one of the most important tracers of the interstellar medium. The HI 21-cm line, also known as the hydrogen hyperfine transition, is produced when the spins of the proton and electron flip from a parallel to an anti-parallel configuration.

The rest frequency of the HI line is 1420.405751 MHz, making it one of the most widely used tracers of galaxy structure and dynamics.

HI observations have been used to map gas distributions in galaxies, measure galaxy rotation curves, study galaxy interactions and provide some of the earliest evidence for dark matter through the unexpectedly flat rotation curves observed in spiral galaxies.

In many nearby galaxies the HI line is observed in emission. However, if a bright radio continuum source lies behind the gas, the HI line can instead be observed in absorption. In this case, foreground atomic hydrogen absorbs a fraction of the continuum emission at the HI transition frequency.

Absorption-line observations are particularly powerful because they allow us to probe gas that may be too faint to detect in emission. The depth, velocity and spatial structure of the absorption provide information about the density, kinematics and distribution of the gas along the line of sight.

For NGC 660, the HI absorption arises against a compact radio source at the galaxy centre. By imaging the absorption as a function of velocity, we can investigate the structure and dynamics of the neutral atomic gas in the nuclear region and search for evidence of gas inflow, outflow or rotation.

Inspect the Data

Before imaging any spectral-line dataset it is important to understand the structure of the observations.

The first step is therefore to inspect the measurement set and determine:

  • The science target.
  • The number of spectral windows.
  • The channel structure.
  • The observing frequency.
  • The antenna configuration.

This information will be useful later when choosing imaging parameters. 

# In CASA

listobs(
    vis='ngc660.ms',
)

Take a few minutes to inspect the output. In particular identify:

  • The field corresponding to NGC 660.
  • The central observing frequency.
  • The total number of channels.

These quantities will be needed throughout the reduction process.

Determine the Channels Containing the Line Feature

Before producing a spectral cube we must determine which channels contain spectral-line absorption. We also need to identify channels that are free of line absorption because these will later be used to fit and subtract the continuum.

A convenient way to do this is to average the visibilities and plot amplitude as a function of channel number. 

# In CASA

plotms(
    vis='ngc660.ms',
    xaxis='channel',
    yaxis='amp',
    avgspw=False,
    avgtime='1e9',
    avgscan=True,
    avgbaseline=True,
    showgui=True,
)

[INSERT PLOTMS SPECTRUM FIGURE]

The spectral line appears as a deviation from the continuum level. Identify the channel range occupied by the absorption feature and determine which channels appear free of absorption on either side of the line.

These line-free channels will be used for continuum subtraction in the next section.

Continuum Subtraction

To study the absorption line, we first need to remove the continuum emission from the data.

We therefore fit a continuum model using the line-free channels identified above and subtract this model directly in the visibility domain.

Performing the subtraction in visibility space is generally preferred because it avoids imaging artefacts and provides a cleaner isolation of the spectral-line signal. 

# In CASA

os.system('rm -rf ngc660.contsub*')

uvcontsub(
    vis='NGC660shift.ms',
    outputvis='NGC660shift.contsub.ms',
    datacolumn='data',
    fitspec='0:100~450;550~900',
    fitorder=1,
    writemodel=False,
)

# In CASA

plotms(
    vis='NGC660shift.contsub.ms',
    xaxis='channel',
    yaxis='real',
    avgspw=False,
    avgtime='1e9',
    avgscan=True,
    avgbaseline=True,
    showgui=True,
)

The continuum-subtracted data should now have an average amplitude close to zero in the line-free channels.

Here we plot the Real component of the visibility rather than the amplitude.

After continuum subtraction, an unresolved absorption feature appears as a negative contribution to the visibility. Plotting the amplitude removes the sign information and can make absorption appear similar to emission.

Try plotting the amplitude instead and compare the resulting spectrum. What information is lost?

You may also experiment with plotting the imaginary component and compare the result with the real component and the amplitude.

Creating a Dirty Cube

Before performing any deconvolution it is often useful to examine the dirty cube.

A dirty cube is produced by Fourier transforming the visibilities without applying any CLEAN iterations. This allows us to:

  • Estimate the image noise.
  • Inspect the quality of the data.
  • Determine which channels contain absorption.
  • Decide on an appropriate cleaning strategy.

The dirty cube therefore serves as an important diagnostic before investing time in a full deconvolution. 

# In CASA

rstfrq='1416.380396911976MHz'

This is the redshifted HI 21-cm frequency corresponding to the redshift of NGC 660.

Velocity Reference Frame

For Galactic sources, it is common to work in the Local Standard of Rest Kinematic (LSRK) frame because it approximately removes the motion of the Sun relative to nearby stars.

NGC 660, however, is an extragalactic source. The recessional velocity of the galaxy is much larger than corrections resulting from the Earth's orbital motion around the Sun.

The barycentric (BARY) frame removes the effect of the Earth's orbital motion and enables observations made at different epochs to be compared consistently.

For this reason we use: 

outframe='BARY'

Why Do We Use Optical Velocity?

Radio spectral-line observations can express velocity using either the radio velocity convention or the optical velocity convention.

For nearby Galactic sources the difference is usually small. For extragalactic sources, however, the distinction becomes more significant and it is important to state explicitly which convention is being used.

Historically, galaxy redshifts and systemic velocities have been reported using the optical velocity convention: 

vopt = c (λ − λ₀) / λ₀

or equivalently: 

vopt = c (ν₀ − ν) / ν

Since NGC 660 is an external galaxy and its literature velocity is usually reported using the optical convention, adopting the same convention allows direct comparison with previously published work.

Throughout this tutorial, velocities should therefore be interpreted as optical velocities measured in the barycentric reference frame.

Dirty Cube Imaging

Unlike the TW Hya dataset, where the line is observed in emission, the HI line in NGC 660 appears in absorption. Therefore genuine signal will appear as negative flux-density features in the cube.

Linux

# In CASA

os.system('rm -rf ngc660.dirty.cube*')

tclean(
    vis='ngc660.contsub.ms',
    imagename='ngc660.dirty.cube',
    spw='0',
    specmode='cube',
    perchanweightdensity=True,
    nchan=400,
    start=300,
    width=1,
    outframe='BARY',
    restfreq=rstfrq,
    deconvolver='hogbom',
    gridder='standard',
    imsize=[320,320],
    cell='0.0018arcsec',
    weighting='briggsbwtaper',
    robust=0.5,
    restoringbeam='common',
    interactive=False,
    niter=0,
    pbcor=True,
)

Inspecting the Cube

The first quantity we would like to measure is the image noise.

The RMS noise provides a quantitative estimate of the sensitivity of the observations and will guide the choice of stopping threshold during deconvolution. 

# In CASA

imstat(
    'ngc660.dirty.cube.image',
    chans='1~100'
)

Measure the RMS noise in several channels that appear free of absorption. The values should be broadly consistent from channel to channel.

Inspecting the Dirty Cube in CARTA

[VIDEO PLACEHOLDER: Inspecting the Dirty Cube in CARTA]

Open the dirty cube in CARTA and inspect the absorption channel by channel.

Questions to consider:

  • In which channels is the absorption visible?
  • Is the absorption compact or extended?
  • Does the morphology change across the profile?
  • Is the absorption detected at high significance?

These observations will guide the cleaning strategy.

A common approach is to clean to approximately three times the RMS noise. 

threshold = 3 × RMS
# In CASA

rms='XXXmJy'
thrshld=3*rms

Producing the Final Cube

Having inspected the dirty cube and identified the absorption channels, we can now perform a full deconvolution.

During the cleaning process we will construct masks around regions of genuine absorption and clean down to a threshold of approximately three times the RMS noise.

Analysing the Final Cube

The final science product is: 

ngc660.final.cube.image.pbcor

Begin by measuring the RMS noise in several line-free channels and compare the values with those measured in the dirty cube.

Next, inspect the cube in CARTA and explore the data both spatially and spectrally:

  • Step through the channels.
  • Extract spectra from different regions.
  • Identify the velocity range of absorption.
  • Examine the spatial distribution as a function of velocity.

These exploratory analyses often yield the first scientific insights from the dataset.

Moment Maps

Moment maps provide a compact way of summarising information contained within a spectral cube.

  • Moment 0: Integrated absorption map.
  • Moment 1: Intensity-weighted velocity of the absorbing gas.
  • Moment 2: Velocity dispersion of the absorbing gas.

Moment maps are powerful but sensitive to noise, making it important to restrict the analysis to the channels containing genuine absorption. 

# In CASA

os.system('rm -rf ngc660.trial.mom')

immoments(
    'ngc660.final.cube.image.pbcor',
    outfile='ngc660.trial.mom',
    moments=[0,1,2],
)

Inspect the resulting moment maps in CARTA.

We can improve the moment maps by restricting the channel range and applying a threshold. 

# In CASA

os.system('rm -rf ngc660.mom')

immoments(
    'ngc660.final.cube.image.pbcor',
    outfile='ngc660.mom',
    includepix=[],
    chans='',
    moments=[0,1,2],
)

Optical Depth

Thus far we have treated the HI absorption in terms of the observed decrease in flux density against the background continuum source.

A more physically meaningful quantity is the optical depth, which measures how effectively the intervening gas absorbs radiation.

Optical-depth measurements are a powerful tool for studying the nuclear regions of active galaxies and can provide insight into the physical conditions and distribution of the absorbing gas.

In the next stage of the analysis we will estimate the optical depth of the HI absorption and investigate what it reveals about the gas in the central region of NGC 660.

Exporting the Results

Although CASA stores images in its native format, FITS remains the standard format used by most astronomical software.

Exporting the continuum image, spectral cube and moment maps to FITS allows them to be archived, shared and analysed in external packages. 

# In CASA

exportfits(
    imagename='ngc660.cont.image',
    fitsimage='ngc660_cont.fits',
    overwrite=True,
)

# In CASA

exportfits(
    imagename='ngc660.final.cube.image.pbcor',
    fitsimage='ngc660_cube.fits',
    velocity=True,
    overwrite=True,
)
